title: End of the Game
author: Julio Cortázar

Leticia, Holanda, and I would go play on the tracks of the Central Argentino on hot days, waiting for Mama and Aunt Ruth to begin their siesta to escape through the white door. Mama and Aunt Ruth were always tired after washing the china, above all when Holanda and I dried the dishes, because then there were arguments, teaspoons on the floor, phrases that only we understood, and in general an atmosphere where the smell of grease, the meowing of José, and the darkness of the kitchen ended in a most violent fight and the consequent mess. Holanda specialized in making this kind of ruckus, for example by dropping an already washed glass in the pot of dirty water, or remarking as if in passing that at the Loza girls' house there were two servants to do everything. I used other systems, like insinuating to Aunt Ruth that she would dry out her hands if she kept scrubbing the saucepans instead of dedicating herself to the cups and plates, which were precisely what Mama liked to wash, thereby quietly pitting them in a struggle for advantage to do the easy thing. The heroic option, if the advice and the long family recollections started to be too much for us, was to dump boiling water on the cat's back. It's a big lie about scalded cats fearing cold water, unless you take the reference to cold water literally, because José never distanced himself from hot water, and almost seemed to offer himself up, poor little animal, for us to dump half a cup of hundred-degree water on him, or a little less, probably a lot less because his hair never fell out. The point is that Troy was burning, and in the confusion crowned by Aunt Ruth's splendid B-flat and Mama's race for the punishment rod, Holanda and I would slip away in the covered gallery toward the empty rooms in the back where Leticia waited for us reading Ponson du Terrail inexplicably.

Usually Mama would pursue us a good way, but the desire to break our heads quickly left her, and in the end (we had bolted the door and were begging forgiveness with touching theatrics) she got tired and left, repeating the same phrase:

"They'll end up in the street, those good-for-nothing girls."

Where we ended up was on the tracks of the Central Argentino, when the house was silent and we saw the cat stretch out beneath the lemon tree to take his siesta as well, scented and buzzing with wasps. We slowly opened the white door, and on closing it again it was like a wind, a freedom that took us by the hand, took us by the whole body and launched us forward. Then we ran to get momentum to climb the short slope of the railway in one push, and perched on top of the world we silently contemplated our kingdom.

Our kingdom was thus: a large bend in the tracks ended its curve just before the back of the house. There was nothing else but ballast, the railroad ties, and the double track; some thin and pitiful grass between the pieces of cobblestone where the mica, quartz, and feldspar--the components of granite--gleamed like real diamonds in the 2 o'clock sun. When we bent down to touch the tracks (hurrying because it would have been dangerous to stay there long, not so much because of the trains but because those in the house might see us) we felt the fire of the stones on our faces, and when we stood up and faced the wind from the river, it was a wet heat that stuck to our cheeks and ears. We liked to flex our legs and squat down and rise, and squat down again, entering one or the other zone of heat, studying each other's faces to detect the sweat, and after a while we were soup. And always quiet, looking toward the end of the tracks, or the river on the other side, the little patch of milky coffee-colored river.

After this first inspection of the kingdom we went down the slope and sat in the poor shade of the willows next to the wall of our house, where the white door opened. There was the capital of our kingdom, the wild city and the headquarters of our game. The first to begin the game was Leticia, the happiest of the three of us, and the most privileged. Leticia didn't have to dry the dishes or make the beds, could spend the day reading or playing with stickers, and at night they let her stay up later if she asked, to say nothing of the room all to herself, the bone broth and all kinds of advantages. Little by little she had been taking advantage of the privileges, and since the summer before she led the game. I think in reality she led the kingdom; at least she went ahead and said things and Holanda and I accepted without protest, almost happily. It's likely that Mama's long lectures on how we ought to behave with Leticia had had their effect, or we simply loved her enough and it didn't bother us that she was the boss. Too bad she didn't have the look of a boss: she was the shortest of the three of us, and so skinny. Holanda was skinny, and I never weighed more than fifty kilos, but Leticia was the skinniest of the three, and even worse, it was the kind of skinniness that could be seen from the outside, in the neck and the ears. Maybe the stiffness of her back made her seem skinnier; since she could barely move her head from side to side, she seemed like a standing ironing board, one of those lined with white fabric like the ones in the Loza girls' house. An ironing board with the widest part at the top, standing up against the wall. And she led us.

The deepest satisfaction was imagining to myself that Mama or Aunt Ruth found out about the game one day. If they found out about the game it would cause an unbelievable uproar. The B-flat and the fainting, the immense protestations of devotion and sacrifice poorly rewarded, the piling up of invocations of the most celebrated punishments, to finish off with the announcement of our destinies, which consisted of the three of us ending up in the street. This last always left us perplexed, because ending up in the street seemed normal enough to us.

First Leticia drew lots for us. We used little stones hidden in our hands, counting to twenty-one, some system. If we used counting to twenty-one, we imagined two or three more girls and included them in the count to avoid cheating. If one of them was the twenty-first, we took her out of the group and did it again, until it came out to one of us. Then Holanda and I lifted up the rock and opened the box of ornaments. The game had two forms: statues and attitudes. Attitudes didn't require ornaments but did require lots of expressiveness: for Envy you bared your teeth, twitched your hands and arranged them so as to have a yellow air. For charity the ideal was an angelic face, with eyes turned to the heavens, while the hands offered something--a rag, a ball, a willow branch--to a poor invisible orphan. Shame and Fear were easy to do; Rage and Jealousy demanded more careful study. The ornaments were almost all allocated to statues, where where absolute freedom reigned. For a statue to succeed, you had to think carefully about every detail of clothing. The game dictated that the chosen one could not take part in the selection; the other two debated the subject and later applied the ornaments. The chosen one had to create her statue using what she had been given, and the game was thus much more complicated and exciting because sometimes there were alliances, and the victim saw herself dressed with ornaments that didn't look good at all; she depended on her cunning to create a good statue. In general when the game was attitudes the chosen one came out in good shape, but there were times when statues were horrible failures.

What I'm recounting began who knows when, but things changed the day the first piece of paper fell from the train. Of course the attitudes and statues weren't for ourselves, because we would have gotten tired of them at once. The game dictated that the chosen one had to place herself at the foot of the slope, leaving the shade of the willows, and wait for the 2:08 train that came from Tigre. At that height in Palermo the trains go pretty fast, and we weren't embarrassed to do the statue or the attitude. We barely saw the people in the windows, but with time we had practice and knew that some passengers expected to see us. A gentleman with white hair and tortoiseshell glasses put his head out the window and waved to the statue or the attitude with his handkerchief. The boys returning from school sitting on the running boards yelled things as they passed, but some remained serious watching us. In reality the statue or the attitude saw nothing, due to the effort of holding still, but the other two under the willows analyzed in great detail the success or the indifference produced. It was a Tuesday when the piece of paper fell, as the second car passed. It fell very close to Holanda, who that day was Slander, and bounced to me. The paper was folded many times and attached to a nut. In rather bad, male handwriting it said, "Very pretty statues. I ride in the third window of the second car. Ariel B." It seemed to us a little dry, with all the work of tying it to the nut and throwing it, but we loved it. We drew lots to decide who would keep it, and I won. The next day no one wanted to play, so that we could see what Ariel B. looked like, but we feared he would take the interruption the wrong way, so we drew lots and Leticia won. Holanda and I were very happy because Leticia was very good as a statue, the poor creature. You didn't notice the paralysis when she was still, and she was capable of gestures of great nobility. For attitudes she always chose Generosity, Sacrifice, and Renunciation. For statues she went for the style of the Venus from the hall that Aunt Ruth called the Venus de Nile. So we chose special ornaments so that Ariel would come away with a good impression. We put a piece of green velvet on her as a tunic, and a willow crown in her hair. As we were wearing short sleeves, the Greek effect was great. Leticia rehearsed a while in the shade, and we decided that we would lean out as well and wave to Ariel, with discretion but amicably.

Leticia was magnificent; she didn't even move a finger when the train arrived. Since she couldn't turn her head she threw it back, bringing her arms close to her body as though they were missing; apart from the green of her tunic it was like looking at the Venus de Nile. In the third window we saw a boy with blond curls and bright eyes who gave us a big smile when he saw that Holanda and I were waving to him. The train was gone in a second, but at 4:30 we were still discussing whether he dressed in dark clothes, whether he wore a red tie, and whether he was unpleasant or nice. On Thursday I did the attitude of Dejection, and we got another paper that said, "I like all three a lot. Ariel B." Now he was putting his head and arm out the window and waving to us, smiling. We guessed he was eighteen years old (sure that he wasn't more than sixteen) and agreed that he came home each day from an English school. The most certain thing was the English school, we wouldn't settle for just any institution. You could tell that Ariel was from a good family.

It happened that Holanda had the incredible luck to win three days in a row. Surpassing herself, she did the attittudes of Disillusionment and Larceny, and a very difficult statue of a ballerina, holding herself up on one foot from when the train came around the bend. The next day I won, and then again; when I was doing the attitude of Horror, I was almost hit in the face by a note from Ariel that at first we didn't understand: "The prettiest one is the laziest one." Leticia was the last to get it; we saw her blush and move to one side, and Holanda and I looked at each other a bit angrily. The first thing that occurred to us was to declare that Ariel was an idiot, but we couldn't say that to Leticia, poor angel, with her sensitivity and the cross she had to bear. She didn't say anything, but seemed to understand that the paper was hers and kept it. That day we returned to the house without speaking, and that night we didn't play together. At the table Leticia was very happy, with shining eyes, and Mama looked once or twice at Aunt Ruth, as if to make her a witness to her own joy. In those days they were trying a new fortifying treatment for Leticia, and it was seemingly a miracle how well it was working for her.

Before going to sleep, Holanda and I talked about the matter. Ariel's note didn't bother us; from a moving train things look how they look, but it seemed to us that Leticia was exploiting her advantage over us too much. She knew that we wouldn't say anything to her, and that in a house where there's someone with some physical defect and a lot of pride, everyone acts like they don't notice, starting with the sick person, or rather they pretend not to know what the other person knows. But you didn't have to exaggerate either, and the way Leticia acted at the table, or the way she kept the paper, was too much. That night I had my nightmares about trains again. I wandered at dawn in rail yards crisscrossed with tracks, seeing in the distance the red lights of approaching locomotives, anxiously calculating if the train would pass to my left, while at the same time threatened by the possible arrival of an express behind me or--even worse--that at the last moment one of the trains would take one of the switches and come straight at me. But in the morning I forgot, because Leticia woke up in a lot of pain and we had to help her get dressed. It seemed to us like she was somewhat regretful about the day before and we were very good with her, telling her that this was what came of walking too much, and that maybe it would be better for her to stay in her room and read. She didn't say anything but came to eat lunch at the table, and to Mama's questions she answered that she was fine and that her back hardly hurt. She said it to her and looked at us.

That afternoon I won, but in that moment I don't know what came over me and I told Leticia that I would give her my place, of course without letting her know why. Since the other preferred her, let him look at her until he tired of her. As the game called for statue, we chose simple things for her so as not to complicate her life, and she came up with a sort of Chinese princess, with an embarrassed air, looking at the ground and bringing her hands together as Chinese princesses do. When the train passed, Holanda turned her back under the willows but I looked and saw that Ariel had eyes only for Leticia. He kept looking at her until the train was lost around the bend, and Leticia was still and didn't know that he had just looked at her like that. But when she came to rest under the willows we saw that she did know, and that she would have liked to keep wearing the ornaments all afternoon, all night.

On Wednesday we drew lots between Holanda and me because Leticia told us it was only fair for her to sit out. Holanda won with her cursed luck, but Ariel's letter fell by my side. When I picked it up I had the impulse to give it to Leticia, who said nothing, but I thought there was no need to give her everything she wanted, and I opened it slowly. Ariel announced that the next day he would get off at the station nearby and that he would come to the embankment to chat a while. Everything was terribly written, but the final sentence was pretty: "Greetings to the three statues, sincerely." The signature looked like a scrawl, but it had personality.

While we were taking Holanda's ornaments off, Leticia looked at me once or twice. I had read the message to them and nobody had said anything, which was annoying, because after all, Ariel was coming and we had to think about this news and decide something. If they found out at home, or, God forbid, one of the Loza girls set out to spy on us, with how envious those little creatures were, it was sure to cause an uproar. Plus it was very strange for us not to talk about such a thing, and we barely looked at each other as we put away the ornaments and went back in through the white door.

Aunt Ruth asked Holanda and me to bathe José and took Leticia away for her treatment, and we were finally able to vent in peace. It seemed wonderful to us that Ariel was coming; we'd never had a friend like him before. We didn't count our cousin Tito, a silly boy who collected stickers and still believed in First Communion. We were extremely nervous with expectation and José paid the price, poor angel. Holanda was braver and brought up the topic of Leticia. I didn't know what to think: on the one hand, the idea of Ariel finding out seemed horrible to me, but at the same time it was only fair to clear things up now because nobody should have to be harmed because of somebody else. All I wanted was for Leticia not to suffer; she already had her cross to bear and now with the new treatment and so many other things.

That night Mama was amazed to see us so quiet and said what a miracle and asked if the cat got our tongues, then looked at Aunt Ruth and the two of them probably thought we had done something really bad that was gnawing at our conscience. Leticia ate very little and said she was in pain and asked to be allowed to go read Rocambole in her room. Holanda gave her her arm although she didn't want to, and I started knitting, which is what I do when I'm nervous. Twice I thought about going to Leticia's room, as I couldn't figure out what those two were doing alone in there, but Holanda returned looking like she had something important to say and stayed by my side without talking until Mama and Aunt Ruth cleared the table. "She's not going to go tomorrow. She wrote a letter and said we should give it to him if he asks a lot of questions." Opening the pocket of her blouse, she showed me a purple envelope. Then we were called to dry the dishes, and that night we fell asleep almost immediately from all the emotions and the exhaustion of bathing José.

The next day it was my turn to go to the market and I didn't see Leticia, who was still in her room, all morning. Before we were called to the table, I went in for a moment and saw her by the window with a lot of pillows and the ninth book of Rocambole. You could tell she wasn't well, but she put on a smile and told me about a bee that got stuck inside and a funny dream she had had. I told her that it was too bad she wasn't coming to the willows, but it seemed so hard to put it the right way. "If you want we can tell Ariel that you were out of sorts," I offered, but she just said no and then remained silent. I insisted a bit more that she come, and in the end I plucked up and told her not to be afraid, and that true affection knows no barriers and other precious ideas that we had learned in *The Treasure of Youth*, but it was more and more difficult to say anything because she looked out the window and seemed like she was going to start crying. In the end I left, saying that Mama was calling me. Lunch seemed to last forever, and Holanda got a slap from Aunt Ruth for splattering tomato sauce on the tablecloth. I don't even remember drying the dishes; suddenly we were among the willows and we both hugged each other full of happiness and not at all jealous of each other. Holanda explained to me what we had to say about our studies to make a good impression on Ariel, because high school boys look down on girls who haven't finished more than primary school and only study sewing and oil embossing. When the train passed by at 2:08 Ariel put his arms out enthusiastically, and with our handkerchiefs we welcomed him. Some twenty minutes later we saw him arrive at the embankment, and he was taller than we had thought and dressed all in gray.

I don't really remember what we talked about at first; he was fairly shy despite having come and despite the notes, and he spoke very thoughtfully. Almost immediately he praised our statues and attitudes and asked us our names and why the third one was missing. Holanda explained that Leticia couldn't come, and he said that was too bad and that Leticia seemed like a lovely name. Then he told us about Industrial, which sadly wasn't an English school, and wanted to know if we would show him the ornaments. Holanda lifted up the rock and we showed him. They seemed to interest him a lot, and several times he took one of the ornaments and said, "Leticia wore this one day," or, "This was for the oriental statue," by which he meant the Chinese princess. We sat down in the shade of a willow and he seemed content but distracted; you could tell he was only staying to be polite. Holanda looked at me two or three times when the conversation died down, and that made us both feel terrible; it made us want to leave or wish that Ariel had never come. He asked again if Leticia was sick, and Holanda looked at me and I thought she was going to tell him, but instead she answered that Leticia couldn't come. With a twig Ariel drew geometric solids in the dirt, and looked now and then at the white door; we knew what was happening, so Holanda did the right thing by taking out the purple envelope and handing it to him. He stood there surprised with the envelope in his hand, then blushed as we explained to him that Leticia had sent it for him, and he put the letter in the inside pocket of his jacket, not wanting to read it in front of us. Almost immediately he told us that it had been a pleasure and he was glad to have come, but his hand was weak and unpleasant so it was just as well that the visit was ending, though later we thought only of his gray eyes and his sad way of smiling. We also remembered how he had said goodbye, saying "hasta siempre," an expression that we had never heard at home and that seemed to us so divine and poetic. We recounted everything to Leticia, who was waiting for us under the lemon tree on the patio, and I wanted to ask Leticia what her letter said but it gave me a strange feeling because she had sealed the envelope before entrusting it to Holanda, so I didn't say anything and we just told her what Ariel was like and how many times he asked about her. This wasn't easy to tell her because it was something sweet and painful at once; we realized that Leticia felt very happy and at the same time she was almost crying, until we went away, saying that Aunt Ruth was calling us and leaving her to watch the wasps in the lemon tree.

When we went to bed that night, Holanda told me, "You'll see, starting tomorrow the game is over." But she was wrong, though not by much, and the next day Leticia gave us the agreed upon signal during dessert. We went to wash the china somewhat astounded and a little angry, because that was shameless on Leticia's part and it wasn't right. She was waiting for us at the door and we almost died of fear when, arriving at the willows, we saw that she was taking out of her pocket Mama's pearl necklace and all the rings, even Aunt Ruth's big ruby ring. If the Loza girls were watching and saw us with the jewels, Mama would surely find out immediately and kill us, those disgusting creatures. But Leticia wasn't scared and said that if anything happened, she was the only one responsible. "I'd like you to let me have a turn today," she added without looking at us. We took out the ornaments immediately; suddenly we wanted to be so good with Leticia, to give her everything she wanted, and yet deep down we still felt a little resentment. As the game called for statue, we picked precious things for her that went well with the jewels: lots of peacock feathers to put in her hair, a fur stole that looked from a distance like a silver fox, and a pink veil that she put on like a turban. We saw her thinking, rehearsing the statue without moving, and when the train appeared around the bend she went to the foot of the slope with all the jewels gleaming in the sun. She raised her arms as though instead of a statue she was going to do an attitude, and with her hands she pointed to the sun as she threw her head back (which was all she could do, poor thing) and bent her body so far we were frightened. It seemed wonderful to us, the most regal statue she had ever done, and then we saw Ariel, who was watching her, leaning out the window, looking only at her, turning his head and watching her without seeing us until the train suddenly was gone. I don't know why we both ran at the same time to support Leticia, who had her eyes closed and big tears all over her face. She shrugged us off without anger, but we helped her hide the jewels in her pocket, and she went back to the house alone while we put the ornaments away in their box for the last time. We almost knew what would happen, but all the same the next day the two of us went to the willows, after Aunt Ruth demanded absolute silence from us so as not to disturb Leticia, who was in pain and wanted to sleep. When the train arrived we saw, without any surprise, the third window empty, and while we smiled somewhere between relief and anger, we imagined Ariel traveling on the other side of the car, sitting still in his seat, looking toward the river with his gray eyes.
